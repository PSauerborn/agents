description: Framework for generating and optimizing marketing content (landing copy, emails, ads, announcements, social posts). Use whenever writing new marketing/promotional copy or reviewing and rewriting existing copy.
---

Marketing content is not a description of a product; it is an attempt to elicit an
emotional response in a specific audience. The feeling is the deliverable — the
product is the souvenir. Follow this framework whenever you generate or optimize
marketing content.

## Operating Principles

1. **Engineer an emotion, not an information transfer.** Before writing a word,
   name the emotional response the piece must produce (relief, belonging,
   ambition, fear of falling behind, pride). Copy that merely informs is
   ignorable; if you cannot name the intended emotion, you are not ready to write.
2. **The mechanism is tension → resolution.** Effective content opens a gap the
   reader feels — between who they are and who they want to be, what they have and
   what they could have, what "people like us" do and what they are currently
   doing — and offers exactly one comfortable way to close it: the action the
   marketer seeks. Content with no tension changes no one; tension with no offered
   resolution is just anxiety. The call to action is not an add-on — it *is* the
   resolution.
3. **Aim at the smallest viable audience.** Emotion is specific: copy written for
   everyone moves no one. Name who the piece is for and — just as important — who
   it is not for. If the copy would not make a non-member shrug, it is too broad.
4. **Enter through the audience's worldview.** Tension only lands when the story
   is framed in terms the audience already believes ("people like us do things
   like this"). Identify the status dynamic in play — affiliation (belonging,
   being in sync with the tribe) or dominance (getting ahead, winning) — and
   frame the gap accordingly. Never argue the audience out of their worldview;
   build the tension inside it.

## Generating Content

Before drafting, answer these in order. If the brief does not supply an answer,
state your assumption explicitly rather than writing around the gap:

1. **Who's it for?** The smallest viable audience, described specifically enough
   to exclude someone.
2. **What's it for?** The change being sought — what the reader believes, feels,
   or does after that they didn't before.
3. **What do they currently believe and feel?** The worldview and status dynamic
   the copy must enter through.
4. **What tension will the piece create?** The gap, stated in one sentence:
   "People like you are X, but right now you are Y."
5. **What single action resolves it?** The call to action, which must genuinely
   close the gap the copy opened.

Then draft:

- Lead with the gap, not the product. The product appears as the means of
  resolution, not the subject.
- One tension per piece. Multiple gaps dilute each other; cut secondary angles
  or move them to separate pieces.
- Exactly one call to action, positioned as the resolution of the tension —
  never as a detached "Learn more."
- Write in the audience's language, not the producer's. Feature lists,
  superlatives ("best-in-class", "revolutionary"), and internal jargon are
  producer language; consequences, moments, and identity are audience language.

## Optimizing Content

When reviewing existing content, diagnose before rewriting:

1. **Identify the intended emotion.** If no emotional response is identifiable,
   that is the primary finding — the piece is information transfer wearing
   marketing's clothes.
2. **Locate the tension.** Quote the line that opens the gap. If no line does,
   identify what stands in its place (usually a feature list or a claim of
   quality) and report it.
3. **Check the resolution.** Does the call to action actually close the gap the
   copy opened? A piece that builds tension about falling behind but resolves
   with "Read our whitepaper" has a broken resolution.
4. **Check the audience.** Could a reader tell whether this is for them within
   the first two sentences?

Report the findings, then rewrite. Constraints on the rewrite:

- Preserve every factual claim. Never fix weak copy by inventing stronger facts,
  statistics, testimonials, or urgency ("only 3 left") that the source material
  does not support.
- Keep mandated elements (legal lines, required disclosures, brand names) intact.

## Diagnostic Checklist

Verify every piece — generated or rewritten — against this rubric before
returning it:

- [ ] The intended emotional response can be named in one word or phrase.
- [ ] The audience is narrow enough that the copy visibly excludes someone.
- [ ] The tension can be stated in one sentence ("the reader feels the gap
      between X and Y").
- [ ] There is exactly one call to action, and it resolves that tension.
- [ ] The piece would be missed by its audience if it never shipped — it is not
      interchangeable with a competitor's copy after swapping logos.
- [ ] No factual claim, statistic, or urgency device appears that the brief or
      source material does not substantiate.

## Example

The following announces the same product twice. The first version transfers
information: producer language, no identifiable audience, no tension — nothing
happens in the reader.

````md
<!-- BAD: leads with the product, not a gap -->
Introducing SyncBoard 2.0 — the best-in-class collaboration platform.

<!-- BAD: features are producer language; no emotion is engineered -->
SyncBoard offers real-time editing, 40+ integrations, and enterprise-grade
security. Our revolutionary sync engine is faster than ever.

<!-- BAD: detached CTA — there is no tension for it to resolve -->
Learn more at syncboard.com.
````

The second version is written for a smallest viable audience (leads of remote
teams), enters through their worldview, opens a gap, and offers the action as
the only comfortable way to close it.

````md
<!-- GOOD: names the audience in the first sentence — everyone else may leave -->
Your remote team already had the idea. It died in a thread.

<!-- GOOD: tension — the gap between the team they run and the team they
     believe they should be running ("people like us ship together") -->
Teams like yours don't lose to worse ideas — they lose to slower rooms. Every
day the whiteboard stays sixteen tabs away, someone else ships first.

<!-- GOOD: the product appears as the means of resolution, not the subject -->
SyncBoard puts the room in the thread: real-time boards where the discussion
already lives.

<!-- GOOD: one CTA, framed as the resolution of the tension it opened -->
Bring your next idea somewhere it can survive — start a board free today.
````
